How this works

Your lease comes first. If it has an early-termination or lease-break clause with a set fee, that is usually the clearest price. Without one, you may owe the rent for every month left. Unpaid rent can go to collections and hurt your credit.

Re-renting. In many states the landlord must make a good faith effort to re-rent the unit and cannot simply charge you for every remaining month. You would then owe rent until the new tenant moves in, plus costs your lease allows. California, for example, sets this out in Civil Code section 1951.2. The rule varies, so check your state's tenant guide at hud.gov/states or your state attorney general. Some cities add their own rules; Berkeley, California bans lease-break fees.

Talk first, in writing. Ask the landlord whether they will let you go for a fee or accept a replacement tenant, and get any agreement in writing. If you sublet, put it in writing with everyone signed, because you may still owe rent if the new tenant doesn't pay.

Military orders. Under the federal Servicemembers Civil Relief Act, servicemembers with orders such as a permanent change of station or a deployment of 90 days or more can end a residential lease without an early-termination penalty. They give written notice with a copy of the orders. On a lease with monthly rent, it ends 30 days after the next rent due date after the notice. Many states also let survivors of domestic violence end a lease early; check your state.

The math. Months are counted from your move-out day to the lease end day, with part of a month counted by its days. Rent for part of a month is worked out by the day (12 months of rent over 365 days). Every amount is an estimate, not a bill. Not legal advice.
