Moving quietly.

For anyone who needs to move without being followed. Take it one step at a time.

What do you need right now?

Talk to someone, any time.

The National Domestic Violence Hotline is free and confidential. They can help you make a safety plan for the move and find programs near you.

Call 1-800-799-7233 Text START to 88788 Chat on thehotline.org

If you're in danger right now, call 911.

Source: The National Domestic Violence Hotline. Checked November 2026.

Keep your new address private.

  • Ask about your state's address confidentiality program. Many states have one. Your mail goes to a safe address and is forwarded to you, so you don't have to give out your new address. Search "address confidentiality program" and your state's name, or see the list of programs by state.
  • Think twice about the post office's change-of-address form. Those addresses go into a national database that can reach marketers, lenders and magazines. A state program may be the safer choice.
  • Give the new address to as few people and companies as you can. Each one is another place it can leak from.
  • Check your phone and accounts. Shared accounts, location sharing and trackers can show where you are. Safety Net explains how to check.

Sources: WomensLaw.org, NNEDV Safety Net. Checked November 2026.

Getting out of your lease.

  • Many states let survivors end a lease early. Connecticut, New Hampshire, New York and Texas are among them. The rules differ: some need a notice letter, a protective order or a police report. Find your state's housing laws on WomensLaw.org.
  • In federally assisted housing, such as public housing or a housing voucher, a federal law (the Violence Against Women Act) protects you from being evicted because of the abuse and lets you ask for an emergency transfer to another unit.
  • Before you sign anything new, a legal aid office or the Hotline can help you check what your lease and your state allow.

Source: WomensLaw.org, housing protections. Checked November 2026. This is general information, not legal advice.

Money for the move.

  • Local domestic violence programs often help with the costs survivors say matter most: transportation, a deposit, and getting by until the first paycheck. The Hotline can connect you to the program near you.
  • FreeFrom works on financial support for survivors, including direct cash. Check their site for what's open now.

Sources: VAWnet, FreeFrom. Checked November 2026.

What to take with you.

If you have to leave in a hurry, these are the things that are hardest to replace:

  • ID and birth certificates, for you and your children
  • Social Security cards, passports, and any immigration papers
  • Money, cards in your name, and bank details
  • A protective order, with copies, if you have one
  • Your lease or deed, car registration and insurance papers
  • Medical and school records
  • Divorce, custody and other legal papers
  • Medications and refills
  • A safe phone and charger, and a list of important numbers
  • Spare keys, a few changes of clothes, photos and anything you couldn't replace

Source: The Hotline, "When you leave". Checked November 2026.

Help with the move itself.

  • Ask a local domestic violence program first. The safest moves are arranged by a program: they screen the volunteers, keep your address private, help with storage, and plan the day around your safety.
  • Pick a day when you won't be interrupted, and don't post the date or your new address anywhere.
  • If you're moving with children across state lines, talk to a legal advocate first. Custody rules can follow you.
