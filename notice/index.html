How this works

Your lease comes first. Most leases say how much notice they need and how to give it. If the lease says nothing, your state's law sets the minimum, and some cities add their own rules.

One full rent period. On a month-to-month rental, many states ask for notice of one full rent period. Legal Aid of West Virginia puts it as two tests: the landlord must get the notice before the next rent is due, and it can only end the tenancy after the end of that next rent period. Minnesota legal aid gives this example: rent due on the 1st, moving out at the end of April, the landlord must get the notice by 11:59 p.m. on March 31.

30 days is not always the same. A notice given on the 20th that says "out in 30 days" usually fails a one-rent-period rule, because it ends the tenancy partway through a rent period. When the lease asks for a number of days, this page counts that many calendar days back from your move-out day, and also shows the date if your notice has to line up with the end of a rent period.

A lease with an end date. Minnesota legal aid says usually no notice is needed to end a fixed-term lease on its end date, but some leases ask for 30 or 60 days' notice first, and some renew automatically unless you give notice. Moving out before the end date is breaking the lease; see what that could cost.

Give it in writing, and keep proof. West Virginia law requires written notice for month-to-month tenancies, and Minnesota legal aid says a written notice is needed to end any periodic lease, even one agreed out loud. The notice counts when the landlord gets it, not when you send it, so mail it early or hand it over and ask for a dated receipt. Keep a copy. If your landlord takes rent for a period after your move-out day, Minnesota legal aid warns the notice can be cancelled.

Dates here are arithmetic, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and city. Check your state's tenant guide at hud.gov/states or your state attorney general, and give notice a few days before the last day if you can.
