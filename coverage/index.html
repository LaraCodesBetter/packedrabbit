How this works

On a move from one state to another, federal rules give you two levels of mover liability. They're not insurance; they're how much the mover is responsible for.

  • Full value protection is what you get unless you sign it away. If something is lost or damaged, the mover chooses to repair it, replace it with an item of like kind and quality, or pay what it costs to replace. It costs extra, and the mover may offer deductibles that lower the price. Ask for the details of their plan in writing.
  • Released value costs nothing extra, but the mover pays no more than 60 cents a pound for each item. You only get it if you sign a statement agreeing to it on the bill of lading or contract.

Things worth more than $100 a pound, like jewelry, silverware, china, furs, antiques and oriental rugs, are "articles of extraordinary value." List each one in writing on the shipping documents, or the mover can limit what it pays for it.

The floor for the whole move. Under full value protection, the lowest value a mover may put on your shipment is $6.00 a pound times its weight. If your things would cost more than that to replace, you can declare a higher value, at an extra cost.

Your own insurance. Check your homeowners or renters policy before you buy anything extra; it may already cover a move. Insurance a mover sells is separate, is regulated by your state, and disputes with that insurer aren't handled under the federal mover rules.

If something breaks, you have 9 months from delivery to file a written claim. Work out your deadline and write the claim letter.

This page covers moves between states with a mover. A move within one state follows that state's rules, which vary. The weights in the list are our own typical figures, not a rule: weigh yours if you can. Amounts are estimates, not a promise of what any mover will pay, and not legal advice.

Sources: FMCSA, Understanding Valuation and Insurance Options (Protect Your Move); 49 CFR Part 375, Appendix A, Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move; 49 CFR 375.203, both via Cornell Legal Information Institute. Typical item weights are our own estimates. Checked November 2026.
